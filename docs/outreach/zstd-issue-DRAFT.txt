TITLE:
Parallel decompression of a single large file into a memory-mapped output (hs_zstd) - results for evaluation

BODY:
Hello zstd maintainers,

I am Michael Hugh Harrington. I have built a small MIT-licensed tool, hs_zstd, that uses libzstd 1.5.6 unmodified to
compress one large file as many independent zstd frames and then decompress those frames on every core at once,
each core writing its frames straight into their final place in a memory-mapped output file. I would be grateful
if you could take a look and tell me whether the approach is useful to zstd, or point out what I have missed.
I have signed the Meta CLA in case any part of it is ever worth contributing.

Code, Windows build, methodology and raw logs:
https://github.com/CombaryMaker/Harringtons_Insights/tree/main/hs_zstd

How it works, briefly:
1. The file is read in 64 KB windows and each window is grouped by simple byte statistics (k-means, at most three
   groups). This grouping is established work (McDaniel and Heydari 2003; Li, Wang, Stolfo and Herzog 2005) and
   only matters for size when the kind of data changes inside the file; --sets 0 turns it off.
2. Each group is cut into blocks (4 or 16 MB) and every block is compressed as an independent zstd frame, in
   parallel, at level 3.
3. A small run-length map and a CRC-32 let the decoder put every byte back. All frames are decoded in parallel,
   directly into the memory-mapped output where a frame maps to one contiguous range.

Results on one desktop (Ryzen 5 7500X3D, 6 cores / 12 threads, NVMe, Windows 11), 4 GB public file (the 12 Silesia
files joined and repeated), 12 threads, median of several runs, every decode checked by SHA-256:

  tool                          1,000,000 bits down to   compress   decode
  zstd -3, one thread                   314,102           14.7 s    3.73 s
  zstd -3 -T12                          314,102            2.9 s    4.25 s
  pzstd -3 -p 12                        315,013            4.1 s    2.85 s
  hs_zstd, 4 MB blocks, 12 jobs         315,391            2.9 s    2.27 s
  hs_zstd, 16 MB blocks, 12 jobs        314,315            3.5 s    2.51 s

hs_zstd's decode times include forcing the output to disk (FlushViewOfFile/FlushFileBuffers); zstd's and pzstd's
do not. Decoding scales to about 4-6 threads and then becomes disk-write bound (4 MB blocks: 7.5 s on 1 thread,
3.2 s on 4, 2.4 s on 12). Decoder private memory is about 130 MB (4 MB blocks) to 300 MB (16 MB), versus about
10 MB for zstd and 160 MB for pzstd. On a 3.2 GB public mixed tar (Linux source, Blender, pdf.js, CSV) hs_zstd
decoded in 1.75 s vs zstd 2.23 s and pzstd 1.92 s, and the grouping made it slightly smaller than both
(244,338 vs 244,965 and 246,879 per million). zstd --long was smaller than everything on that tar (233,113).

What I think is and is not new: independent frames and parallel decoding are what pzstd and the seekable format
already do, and the grouping is prior work. The part that seems to carry the speed is decoding each frame
directly into its final location in a memory-mapped output instead of through per-thread buffers and an ordered
writer. If that is already known or tried, I would be glad to learn why it was not adopted.

Limits: the .hsz container needs hs_zstd to reassemble (each block inside is a standard zstd frame); memory is
higher than zstd's; all numbers come from one Windows machine; repeats farther apart than one block are lost.

Credit to Andrew Goodwin (University of Sydney), whose review supplied the plain-blocks control, two bug fixes and
the fair-baseline method used here.
